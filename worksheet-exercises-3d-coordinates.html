    Worksheet 22.5 Exercises for Three-Dimensional Coordinate Systems
    

    These problems use the distance formula in space and describe regions bounded by spheres. If you would like to review this topic before attempting them, see Three-Dimensional Coordinate Systems.
    🔗

    1.

    1. Find an equation of the set of all points equidistant from the points \(A(-1,5,3)\) and \(B(6,2,-2)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Describe the geometrical shape corresponding to the set.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We want to find all points \(P(x,y,z)\) that have the same distance from \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\) Squaring both distances (which is equivalent, since distances are non-negative) gives
      \begin{equation*} (x+1)^2 + (y-5)^2 + (z-3)^2 = (x-6)^2 + (y-2)^2 + (z+2)^2. \end{equation*}
      Expanding both sides,
      \begin{align*} \amp x^2 + 2x + 1 + y^2 - 10y + 25 + z^2 - 6z + 9\\ \amp = x^2 - 12x + 36 + y^2 - 4y + 4 + z^2 + 4z + 4,\\ \amp 2x - 10y - 6z + 35 = -12x - 4y + 4z + 44, \end{align*}
      and collecting terms,
      \begin{equation*} 14x - 6y - 10z = 9. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. This is the equation of a plane. Its normal vector is \(\langle 14, -6, -10\rangle = 2\langle 7, -3, -5\rangle\text{,}\) which is parallel to \(\vec{AB} = \langle 6-(-1),\, 2-5,\, -2-3\rangle = \langle 7, -3, -5\rangle\text{.}\) Moreover, the midpoint of \(\overline{AB}\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} C\left(\frac{-1+6}{2},\, \frac{5+2}{2},\, \frac{3+(-2)}{2}\right) = C\left(\frac52, \frac72, \frac12\right), \end{equation*}
      satisfies the equation, since \(14\left(\frac52\right) - 6\left(\frac72\right) - 10\left(\frac12\right) = 35 - 21 - 5 = 9\text{.}\) Hence the set is the plane that passes through the midpoint of \(\overline{AB}\) and is orthogonal to \(\vec{AB}\text{,}\) i.e. the perpendicular bisector plane of the segment \(\overline{AB}\text{.}\) See Figure 22.8.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Describe the following regions and graphs.
    1. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 4\text{,}\) \(z \ge 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 4\text{,}\) \(z = 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\text{,}\) \(z \ge 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\text{,}\) \(z = 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The key is to notice whether the first condition is an inequality (a solid region) or an equation (a surface), and then what the second condition does to it.
    1. The solid ball of radius \(2\) centered at the origin, restricted to \(z \ge 0\text{:}\) the upper half of the solid ball, including the flat disk in the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Setting \(z = 0\) in the solid ball gives \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\text{:}\) the solid disk of radius \(2\) centered at the origin, lying in the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The sphere of radius \(2\) restricted to \(z \ge 0\text{:}\) the upper hemisphere (a surface only), together with the circle where it meets the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Setting \(z = 0\) on the sphere gives \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{:}\) the circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin in the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Consider the point \((3,7,-5)\text{.}\)
    1. What is its distance to the \(yz\)-plane?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What is its distance to the \(y\)-axis?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The \(yz\)-plane is the plane \(x = 0\text{.}\) The closest point of that plane to \((3,7,-5)\) is obtained by simply setting the \(x\)-coordinate to zero, giving \((0,7,-5)\text{.}\) Hence the distance is
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(3-0)^2 + (7-7)^2 + (-5+5)^2} = |3| = 3 . \end{equation*}
    In general the distance from \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\) to the \(yz\)-plane is \(|x_0|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) The \(y\)-axis consists of the points \((0,y,0)\text{.}\) The closest such point to \((3,7,-5)\) is the one with the same \(y\)-coordinate, namely \((0,7,0)\text{,}\) so the distance is
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{(3-0)^2 + (7-7)^2 + (-5-0)^2} = \sqrt{9+25} = \sqrt{34} . \end{equation*}
    In general the distance from \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\) to the \(y\)-axis is \(\sqrt{x_0^2+z_0^2}\text{:}\) we drop the coordinate along the axis and keep the other two. See Figure 22.9.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Describing More Equations and Inequalities Geometrically.

    Describe the geometrical meaning of the following equalities and inequalities.
    1. \(\displaystyle y = -2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle y = -2,\; z = 1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 + z^2 = 9\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 + z^2 \le 9,\; y \ge -1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle y^2 + z^2 = 4\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. \(\displaystyle y^2 + z^2 = 4,\; 0 \le x \le 5\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. \(\displaystyle x = y\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A. This is the set of all points whose \(y\)-coordinate equals \(-2\text{,}\) that is \(\{(x,y,z) \in \R^3 : y = -2\}\text{.}\) Both \(x\) and \(z\) are unrestricted, so it is a plane parallel to the \(xz\)-plane, sitting two units to the negative side of it, shown in Figure 22.10.
    🔗
    B. This is the intersection of the two planes \(y = -2\) and \(z = 1\text{,}\) which is a line. Only \(x\) is unrestricted, so the line is parallel to the \(x\)-axis and passes through the point \((0,-2,1)\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 22.11.
    🔗
    C. Comparing with the standard equation of a sphere, this is the sphere of radius \(3\) centered at \((2,-1,0)\text{.}\) Note the center lies in the \(xy\)-plane, and since the radius is \(3\) the sphere reaches from \(z = -3\) up to \(z = 3\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 22.12.
    🔗
    D. Replacing the equality by \(\le\) gives the solid ball: every point whose distance from \((2,-1,0)\) is at most \(3\text{.}\) The extra condition \(y \ge -1\) keeps only the points on one side of the plane \(y = -1\text{.}\) That plane passes through the center of the ball, so exactly half of the ball survives: the region is a solid half-ball of radius \(3\text{,}\) whose flat face is the disk of radius \(3\) cut out of the plane \(y = -1\text{,}\) shown in Figure 22.13.
    🔗
    E. In the \(yz\)-plane, \(y^2 + z^2 = 4\) is a circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin. In space \(x\) is unrestricted, so the equation represents an infinitely long cylinder of radius \(2\) whose axis is the \(x\)-axis. Compare this with Example 6.10, where the missing variable was \(z\) and the axis was therefore the \(z\)-axis: the axis of the cylinder is always the axis of the variable that does not appear. The left half of Figure 22.14 shows this cylinder.
    🔗
    F. The extra condition \(0 \le x \le 5\) cuts the cylinder of part E down to a piece of length \(5\text{,}\) running from the circle in the plane \(x = 0\) to the circle in the plane \(x = 5\text{,}\) as on the right of Figure 22.14. It is a tube, not a solid: the two end disks are not included, since the equation forces \(y^2 + z^2 = 4\) exactly.
    🔗
    G. The condition \(x = y\) says nothing about \(z\text{,}\) so whenever a point satisfies it, so does the whole vertical line through that point. In the \(xy\)-plane the equation \(x = y\) describes a line through the origin, and sweeping that line vertically gives a plane: the plane containing the \(z\)-axis that cuts the \(xy\)-plane along the line \(y = x\text{,}\) shown in Figure 22.15. It is not parallel to any coordinate plane, unlike part A.
    🔗

    5. A Solid Bounded by a Cylinder and a Plane.

    Consider the solid region bounded by the three surfaces
    \begin{equation*} z = 0, \qquad x^2+y^2 = 4, \qquad x+y+z = 4, \end{equation*}
    restricted to \(z \ge 0\text{.}\) Describe each of the three bounding surfaces, and describe the solid region they enclose.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The equation \(x^2+y^2=4\) is missing \(z\text{,}\) so it is the infinite cylinder of radius \(2\) whose axis is the \(z\)-axis. The equation \(z=0\) is the \(xy\)-plane, and \(x+y+z=4\) is a plane tilted with respect to all three coordinate axes.
    🔗
    Solving the third equation for \(z\) gives \(z = 4-x-y\text{.}\) Over the disk \(x^2+y^2 \le 4\) enclosed by the cylinder, this height stays positive: the largest value of \(x+y\) on that disk is \(2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83\text{,}\) attained in the direction \((1,1)\text{,}\) so \(z = 4-x-y \ge 4-2\sqrt2 > 0\) throughout the disk. The tilted plane therefore never dips below the \(xy\)-plane over the region enclosed by the cylinder.
    🔗
    The solid is the piece of the solid cylinder \(x^2+y^2 \le 4\) lying between the flat base \(z=0\) and the slanted lid \(z=4-x-y\text{:}\) an oblique, truncated cylinder whose base is the disk of radius \(2\) in the \(xy\)-plane and whose slanted top is an ellipse, shown in Figure 22.16.

    6. Graphing a Parabolic Cylinder.

    Sketch the graph of \(z = 4 - x^2\) over the rectangle \(-2 \le x \le 2\text{,}\) \(0 \le y \le 4\) in the \(xy\)-plane. Why is this surface called a parabolic cylinder?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the equation \(z=4-x^2\) does not involve \(y\text{,}\) every cross-section of the surface at a fixed value of \(y\) is the same parabola \(z=4-x^2\) in the \(xz\)-plane, opening downward from its vertex at \((0,4)\text{.}\) Sliding that parabola along the \(y\)-axis from \(y=0\) to \(y=4\) sweeps out the surface.
    🔗
    Over \(-2\le x\le 2\text{,}\) the parabola satisfies \(4-x^2 \ge 0\text{,}\) with equality exactly at \(x=\pm 2\text{,}\) so the surface touches the \(xy\)-plane along the two edges \(x=\pm2\) of the rectangular region and rises to a maximum height of \(4\) along the ridge \(x=0\text{.}\) Because it is generated by sliding a fixed curve along a straight line, rather than curving in the \(y\) direction as well, it is called a parabolic cylinder, shown in Figure 22.17.
    🔗
    🔗

    7. A Solid Bounded by a Paraboloid, a Plane, and a Cylinder.

    Consider the solid region bounded below by the paraboloid \(z = 1-x^2-y^2\text{,}\) above by the plane \(z=1\text{,}\) and on the outside by the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=1\text{.}\) Describe this solid.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The paraboloid \(z=1-x^2-y^2\) opens downward from its vertex \((0,0,1)\text{,}\) reaching \(z=0\) exactly on the circle \(x^2+y^2=1\text{,}\) the same circle where the cylinder meets the \(xy\)-plane. The plane \(z=1\) is tangent to the paraboloid at its vertex.
    🔗
    The solid consists of the points inside the cylinder that lie above the paraboloid and below the plane:
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2 \le 1, \qquad 1-x^2-y^2 \le z \le 1. \end{equation*}
    At the center \((x,y)=(0,0)\text{,}\) this range for \(z\) collapses to the single point \(z=1\text{,}\) since the paraboloid already reaches the plane there. At the rim \(x^2+y^2=1\text{,}\) the range is the full interval \(0 \le z \le 1\text{.}\) The solid is therefore a disk-shaped shell that thins to a single point at its center, as though a dome-shaped cavity bounded by the paraboloid had been hollowed out from the top of the solid cylinder \(x^2+y^2\le1\text{,}\) \(0\le z\le 1\text{,}\) shown in Figure 22.18.

    8. A Solid Bounded by a Cylinder and a Paraboloid.

    Consider the solid bounded by the three surfaces
    \begin{equation*} z = 0, \qquad z = x^2+y^2, \qquad x^2+y^2 = 4. \end{equation*}
    1. Describe each of the three bounding surfaces.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Describe the solid region they enclose.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Describe the cross-section of the solid in the plane \(y=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The equation \(z=0\) is the \(xy\)-plane. The equation \(z=x^2+y^2\) is a paraboloid opening upward from its vertex at the origin. The equation \(x^2+y^2=4\) is missing \(z\text{,}\) so it is the infinite cylinder of radius \(2\) whose axis is the \(z\)-axis. The paraboloid meets that cylinder exactly where \(x^2+y^2=4\text{,}\) at height \(z=4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) The solid consists of the points inside the cylinder lying between the flat base and the paraboloid:
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2 \le 4, \qquad 0 \le z \le x^2+y^2. \end{equation*}
    Near the \(z\)-axis this range for \(z\) is almost nothing, since \(x^2+y^2 \approx 0\) there, while at the rim \(x^2+y^2=4\) it is the full interval \(0 \le z \le 4\text{.}\) The solid is therefore shaped like a shallow bowl carved out from underneath the paraboloid: thin at the center and rising to its full height \(4\) at the cylinder wall, shown in Figure 22.20.
    🔗
    (c) Setting \(y=0\) restricts the solid’s defining inequalities to \(-2\le x\le 2\) and \(0\le z\le x^2\text{:}\) the region under the parabola \(z=x^2\text{,}\) above the \(x\)-axis, between \(x=-2\) and \(x=2\text{,}\) shown in Figure 22.19.
