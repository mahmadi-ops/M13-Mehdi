    Worksheet 22.9 Exercises for Parametrization of Plane Curves
    

    If you would like to review this topic before attempting the exercises, click Parametrization of Plane Curves.
    🔗
    The exercises below on the velocity and direction of a projectile after a given time use the figure of a projectile at a general instant.
    🔗

    1.

    A particle is projected from \(O\) with speed \(v_0\) at an elevation \(\theta\) to the horizontal, as in Figure 22.33. At time \(t\) it is at the point \(P\text{,}\) moving with speed \(v\) in a direction making an angle \(\phi\) with the horizontal. Show that
    \begin{equation*} v = \sqrt{\,v_0^2 - 2\,v_0\,g\,t\sin\theta + g^2 t^2\,} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \tan\phi = \frac{v_0\sin\theta - g\,t}{v_0\cos\theta}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Take the origin at \(O\) with horizontal and vertical axes. The coordinates of \(P\) at time \(t\) are
    \begin{equation*} x = v_0\cos\theta\;t\text{,} \qquad y = v_0\sin\theta\;t - \tfrac{1}{2}g\,t^2\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The horizontal and vertical components of the velocity are the time derivatives of these, so
    \begin{equation*} v\cos\phi = \frac{dx}{dt} = v_0\cos\theta\text{,} \qquad v\sin\phi = \frac{dy}{dt} = v_0\sin\theta - g\,t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Squaring and adding eliminates \(\phi\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} v^2 = v_0^2\cos^2\theta + \bigl(v_0\sin\theta - g\,t\bigr)^2 = v_0^2 - 2\,v_0\,g\,t\sin\theta + g^2 t^2\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which gives the speed at time \(t\text{.}\) Dividing the vertical component by the horizontal component gives the direction,
    \begin{equation*} \tan\phi = \frac{v_0\sin\theta - g\,t}{v_0\cos\theta}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    that is, \(\phi = \arctan\!\left(\dfrac{v_0\sin\theta - g\,t}{v_0\cos\theta}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    The directrix of the parabolic path is the horizontal line at height \(\dfrac{v_0^2}{2g}\) above \(O\text{.}\) Using the previous exercise, show that the speed of the particle at \(P\) is equal to the speed it would acquire by falling freely from the level of the directrix down to \(P\text{.}\) That is, if \(h\) denotes the depth of \(P\) below the directrix (see Figure 22.33), then \(v^2 = 2\,g\,h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the previous exercise,
    \begin{equation*} v^2 = v_0^2 - 2\,v_0\,g\,t\sin\theta + g^2 t^2 = v_0^2 - 2g\!\left(v_0\,t\sin\theta - \tfrac{1}{2}g\,t^2\right) = v_0^2 - 2 g y\text{,} \end{equation*}
    since \(y = v_0\,t\sin\theta - \tfrac{1}{2}g\,t^2\) is the height of \(P\text{.}\) Writing this as
    \begin{equation*} v^2 = 2g\!\left(\frac{v_0^2}{2g} - y\right) \end{equation*}
    and recognizing \(\dfrac{v_0^2}{2g}\) as the height of the directrix above \(O\text{,}\) the quantity \(h = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2g} - y\) is exactly the depth of \(P\) below the directrix. Hence \(v^2 = 2\,g\,h\text{,}\) the square of the speed gained by falling freely through the height \(h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. A collision on the directrix.

    Two objects move under gravity. The first is the projectile of the two previous exercises: launched from \(O\) with speed \(v_0\) at an elevation \(\theta\) to the horizontal. The second is a ball released from rest at a point \(B\) on the directrix, the horizontal line at height \(\dfrac{v_0^2}{2g}\) above \(O\text{.}\) We want the two to collide at the moment the projectile is at half of its maximum height, on the way down. See Figure 22.34.
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that the projectile is at half of its maximum height at the two times
    \begin{equation*} t_\pm = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}\left(1 \pm \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) \end{equation*}
    and say which of these is on the descending branch.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vertical motion is \(y = v_0\sin\theta\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2\text{.}\) It is greatest when \(\dot y = v_0\sin\theta - g t = 0\text{,}\) that is at \(t = v_0\sin\theta/g\text{,}\) which gives the maximum height
    \begin{equation*} H = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Setting \(y = H/2\) gives the quadratic equation
    \begin{equation*} t^2 - \frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}\,t + \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g^2} = 0\text{,} \end{equation*}
    whose discriminant is \(2v_0^2\sin^2\theta/g^2\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} t_\pm = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g} \pm \frac{\sqrt2\,v_0\sin\theta}{2g} = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}\left(1 \pm \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    These straddle the apex time \(v_0\sin\theta/g\) symmetrically, as they must. The descending crossing is the later one,
    \begin{equation*} T = t_+ = \frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)v_0\sin\theta}{2g}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the point \(B\) on the directrix from which the ball must be released.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Released from rest, the ball has no horizontal velocity, so it falls along a vertical line: \(B\) must be directly above the collision point \(C\text{.}\) The projectile’s horizontal coordinate at time \(T\) is
    \begin{equation*} x_C = v_0\cos\theta\cdot T = \frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{4g}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    so the release point is
    \begin{equation*} B = \left(\frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{4g},\; \frac{v_0^2}{2g}\right)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    In terms of the range \(R = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\) this is \(x_C = \frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}R \approx 0.854\,R\)—comfortably past the apex at \(R/2\) and short of the landing point, as Figure 22.34 shows.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find how long after the projectile is launched the ball must be released.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The depth of \(C\) below the directrix is
    \begin{equation*} h = \frac{v_0^2}{2g} - \frac{H}{2} = \frac{v_0^2\left(2-\sin^2\theta\right)}{4g}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Falling from rest through \(h\) takes a time \(\tau\) with \(h = \tfrac12 g\tau^2\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \tau = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \frac{v_0}{g}\sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The ball must therefore be released
    \begin{equation*} t_{\text{rel}} = T - \tau = \frac{v_0}{g}\left[\frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)\sin\theta}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\right] \end{equation*}
    seconds after the projectile is launched.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Show that at the instant of the collision the two objects have exactly the same speed.
    🔗
    Solution.
    By Worksheet Exercise 22.9.2, a projectile at depth \(h\) below its directrix has speed \(v_{\text{proj}} = \sqrt{2gh}\text{.}\) The ball starts from rest on the directrix and has fallen the same depth \(h\text{,}\) so its speed is
    \begin{equation*} v_{\text{ball}} = g\tau = g\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \sqrt{2gh}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The two are equal. This is not a coincidence of our particular choice of \(C\text{:}\) the projectile always carries exactly the speed it would have gained by falling freely from the directrix, and the ball is doing precisely that fall. Wherever on the path we arrange the collision, the two objects meet with the same speed, here
    \begin{equation*} v = \sqrt{2gh} = v_0\sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Their directions of course differ: the ball moves straight down, while the projectile moves down and forward at the angle \(\phi\) of Worksheet Exercise 22.9.1.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Is \(t_{\text{rel}}\) always positive? Find the elevations \(\theta\) for which the ball is released after the projectile is launched, and explain what a negative \(t_{\text{rel}}\) would mean.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Requiring \(t_{\text{rel}} \geq 0\) means
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\left(2+\sqrt2\right)\sin\theta}{2} \geq \sqrt{\frac{2-\sin^2\theta}{2}}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    and squaring gives \(\sin^2\theta\left(4+2\sqrt2\right) \geq 2\text{,}\) that is
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2\theta \geq \frac{1}{2+\sqrt2} = \frac{2-\sqrt2}{2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \theta \geq \arcsin\sqrt{\frac{2-\sqrt2}{2}} \approx 32.8^\circ\text{.} \end{equation*}
    For flatter launches the required release time is negative: the ball would have to be dropped before the projectile is fired, because a shallow trajectory reaches its (low) half-height point sooner than the ball can fall the long way down from the directrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Figure 22.35 puts the whole scenario in motion: the projectile rises to \(H\text{,}\) the ball is released from \(B\) at the time \(t_{\text{rel}}\text{,}\) the two meet at \(C\text{,}\) and the velocity arrows there have the same length.

    4.

    Find the Cartesian equation \(f(x,y) = 0\) for the curve represented by the following parametric equations, then graph it:
    \begin{align*} x(t) \amp = e^{2t},\\ y(t) \amp = e^{t} - 1,\\ 0 \amp \le t \le \ln(2). \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the second equation, \(y + 1 = e^t\text{,}\) and hence
    \begin{equation*} x = e^{2t} = \left(e^{t}\right)^2 = (y+1)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; y + 1 = \sqrt{x} \;\Rightarrow\; y = \sqrt{x} - 1. \end{equation*}
    For the range, note that \(0 \le t \le \ln(2)\) gives
    \begin{equation*} e^0 \le e^t \le e^{\ln(2)} \;\Rightarrow\; 1 \le e^t \le 2 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 \le e^t - 1 \le 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 \le y \le 1, \end{equation*}
    and correspondingly \(1 \le x \le 4\text{.}\) The curve is the portion of \(y = \sqrt{x} - 1\) from \((1,0)\) to \((4,1)\text{,}\) shown in Figure 22.36.
    🔗

    5.

    Provide a parametrized form of the curve given by
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + 4y^2 + 8y - 2\sqrt{21}\,x = 0. \end{equation*}
    Assume that the curve is traced counter-clockwise as the parameter increases.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We complete the squares:
    \begin{align*} \left(x - \sqrt{21}\right)^2 - 21 + 4(y+1)^2 - 4 \amp = 0\\ \left(x - \sqrt{21}\right)^2 + 4(y+1)^2 \amp = 25. \end{align*}
    This is an ellipse centered at \(\left(\sqrt{21}, -1\right)\text{.}\) Setting
    \begin{equation*} x - \sqrt{21} = 5\cos t \quad\text{and}\quad 2(y+1) = 5\sin t \end{equation*}
    gives the counter-clockwise parametrization
    \begin{align*} x(t) \amp = 5\cos t + \sqrt{21}\\ y(t) \amp = \frac52 \sin t - 1\\ 0 \amp \le t \le 2\pi. \end{align*}
    See Figure 22.37.

    🔗

    6.

    An airplane moves in a trajectory given by the parametric equations
    \begin{equation*} x = 4t, \qquad y = t, \qquad z = t^2. \end{equation*}
    Given this trajectory, the airplane will intersect the surface \(z = x - y^2 + 16\) twice. Determine the tangent plane to the surface at the location where the airplane first crosses the surface.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We first find the crossing times by substituting the trajectory into the equation of the surface:
    \begin{align*} t^2 \amp = 4t - t^2 + 16 \;\Rightarrow\; 2t^2 - 4t - 16 = 0\\ \amp \;\Rightarrow\; t^2 - 2t - 8 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (t-4)(t+2) = 0, \end{align*}
    so \(t = -2\) or \(t = 4\text{.}\) The first crossing occurs at the earlier time \(t = -2\text{,}\) at the point
    \begin{equation*} x = 4(-2) = -8, \qquad y = -2, \qquad z = (-2)^2 = 4, \end{equation*}
    i.e. at \(P_0(-8, -2, 4)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Writing the surface as a level surface \(F(x,y,z) = x - y^2 + 16 - z = 0\text{,}\) the normal vector is
    \begin{equation*} \vec n = \nabla F = \langle 1, -2y, -1\rangle, \qquad \vec n\big|_{P_0} = \langle 1, 4, -1\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Hence the tangent plane at \(P_0\) is
    \begin{align*} 1(x+8) + 4(y+2) - 1(z-4) \amp = 0\\ \amp \;\Rightarrow\; x + 4y - z + 20 = 0,\\ \amp \;\Rightarrow\; z = x + 4y + 20. \end{align*}
    As a check, at \((-8,-2)\) this gives \(z = -8 - 8 + 20 = 4\text{,}\) which is the correct height.
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

      🔗
      🔗
    2. Which axis is perpendicular to the plane \(x = 4\text{?}\)
      🔗

    🔗

      🔗

    2. The plane \(x = 4\) has normal vector \(\langle 1, 0, 0\rangle\text{,}\) so the \(x\)-axis is perpendicular to it.

    8.

    Consider the parametrization \(x = 2 - t - t^3\) and \(y = 3t^2 - 6t + 1\text{.}\) Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) at the point with \((x,y)\)-coordinates \((0,-2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First find the parameter value. From \(y = -2\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} 3t^2 - 6t + 1 = -2 \;\Rightarrow\; 3t^2 - 6t + 3 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; t^2 - 2t + 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; t = 1, \end{equation*}
    and this checks out in the first equation: \(x = 2 - 1 - 1 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For a parametrized curve, \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = 6t - 6, \qquad \frac{dx}{dt} = -1 - 3t^2. \end{equation*}
    At \(t = 1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{6(1) - 6}{-1 - 3(1)} = \frac{0}{-4} = 0. \end{equation*}
    The tangent line there is horizontal.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Parametrize the intersection of the surfaces \(4x^2 + z^2 = 16\) and \(y = z\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The first surface does not involve \(y\text{;}\) dividing by \(16\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{z^2}{16} = 1, \end{equation*}
    which is an ellipse in the \(xz\)-plane with semi-axes \(2\) (in \(x\)) and \(4\) (in \(z\)). It is parametrized by \(x = 2\cos(t)\text{,}\) \(z = 4\sin(t)\text{.}\) The second surface simply forces \(y = z\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} x = 2\cos(t), \qquad y = 4\sin(t), \qquad z = 4\sin(t), \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    Consider the curve \(25(y+4)^2 + 400(x-1)^2 = 100\text{.}\)
    1. Sketch the curve and label its important points.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Parametrize the entire curve using one parametrization.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Divide both sides by \(100\) to put the equation in standard form:
    \begin{align*} \frac{25(y+4)^2}{100} + \frac{400(x-1)^2}{100} \amp = 1\\ \frac{(y+4)^2}{4} + 4(x-1)^2 \amp = 1\\ \frac{(x-1)^2}{\left(\frac12\right)^2} + \frac{(y+4)^2}{2^2} \amp = 1 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is an ellipse with centre \((1,-4)\text{,}\) semi-axis \(a = \tfrac12\) in the \(x\)-direction and semi-axis \(b = 2\) in the \(y\)-direction. Since \(b \gt a\text{,}\) the major axis is vertical. The four vertices are
    \begin{equation*} \left(\tfrac12, -4\right), \quad \left(\tfrac32, -4\right), \quad (1,-2), \quad (1,-6), \end{equation*}
    and the foci lie on the vertical axis at distance \(c = \sqrt{b^2-a^2} = \sqrt{4-\tfrac14} = \tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{2} \approx 1.94\) from the centre. See Figure 22.38.
    🔗
    (b) For a circle \(x^2+y^2=r^2\) we would use \(x = r\cos t\text{,}\) \(y = r\sin t\text{.}\) Here the standard form tells us that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x-1}{\frac12} \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{y+4}{2} \end{equation*}
    play the roles of \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\text{,}\) since their squares add to \(1\text{.}\) So we set
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x-1}{\frac12} = \cos t, \qquad \frac{y+4}{2} = \sin t , \end{equation*}
    which gives
    \begin{equation*} x = 1 + \frac12\cos t, \qquad y = -4 + 2\sin t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi . \end{equation*}
    Letting \(t\) run over a full period of length \(2\pi\) traces the entire ellipse exactly once, counterclockwise, starting at the right vertex \(\left(\tfrac32,-4\right)\text{.}\)
