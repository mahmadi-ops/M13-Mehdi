    Worksheet 22.8 Exercises for The Cross Product
    

    These problems use the cross product and the triple product. If you would like to review this topic before attempting them, see The Cross Product.
    🔗

    1. True or False.

      If \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) are two non-zero vectors in \(\R^3\text{,}\) then \(\vec u \cdot (\vec u \times \vec v) = \|\vec u\|^2 + \vec u \cdot \vec v\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • Counterexample: take \(\vec u = \mathbf i\) and \(\vec v = \mathbf i\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf i \cdot \underbrace{(\mathbf i \times \mathbf i)}_{\vec 0} = 0, \quad\text{whereas}\quad \|\mathbf i\|^2 + \mathbf i \cdot \mathbf i = 1 + 1 = 2, \end{equation*}
      and \(0 \ne 2\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • Counterexample: take \(\vec u = \mathbf i\) and \(\vec v = \mathbf i\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf i \cdot \underbrace{(\mathbf i \times \mathbf i)}_{\vec 0} = 0, \quad\text{whereas}\quad \|\mathbf i\|^2 + \mathbf i \cdot \mathbf i = 1 + 1 = 2, \end{equation*}
      and \(0 \ne 2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    2.

    Let \(\mathbf v\) be the vector \(3\,\mathbf i - 4\,\mathbf j + 5\,\mathbf k\text{.}\)
    1. Find a non-zero vector \(\mathbf v'\) orthogonal to \(\mathbf v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find a vector orthogonal to both \(\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf v'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We need \(\langle a,b,c\rangle\) with \(3a - 4b + 5c = 0\text{.}\) Any solution will do; taking \(c = 0\) leaves \(3a = 4b\text{,}\) so \(a = 4\text{,}\) \(b = 3\) works:
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf v' = 4\,\mathbf i + 3\,\mathbf j, \qquad \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v' = 12 - 12 + 0 = 0. \end{equation*}
      (There are infinitely many correct answers.)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The cross product is orthogonal to both:
      \begin{align*} \mathbf v \times \mathbf v' \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 3 \amp -4 \amp 5 \\ 4 \amp 3 \amp 0 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = (0 - 15)\mathbf i - (0 - 20)\mathbf j + (9 + 16)\mathbf k\\ \amp = -15\,\mathbf i + 20\,\mathbf j + 25\,\mathbf k. \end{align*}
      Dividing by \(5\) gives the simpler answer \(-3\,\mathbf i + 4\,\mathbf j + 5\,\mathbf k\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    1. Find the area of the triangle with vertices \((0,1,1)\text{,}\) \((-1,2,4)\text{,}\) \((3,0,1)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the equation of the plane containing the triangle.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Take \(A(0,1,1)\) as the base vertex and form two edge vectors:
    \begin{equation*} \vec a = \vec{AB} = \langle -1, 1, 3\rangle, \qquad \vec b = \vec{AC} = \langle 3, -1, 0\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Their cross product is
    \begin{equation*} \vec a \times \vec b = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ -1 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 3 \amp -1 \amp 0 \end{vmatrix} = (0+3)\mathbf i - (0-9)\mathbf j + (1-3)\mathbf k = \langle 3, 9, -2\rangle. \end{equation*}
    1. The magnitude of the cross product is the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\vec a\) and \(\vec b\text{,}\) so the triangle has half that area:
      \begin{equation*} \text{Area} = \frac12\left|\vec a \times \vec b\right| = \frac12\sqrt{9 + 81 + 4} = \frac{\sqrt{94}}{2}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The cross product is normal to the plane, so the plane has the form \(3x + 9y - 2z = D\text{.}\) Substituting the point \((0,1,1)\) gives \(D = 0 + 9 - 2 = 7\text{,}\) hence
      \begin{equation*} 3x + 9y - 2z = 7. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Suppose \(\|\vec A\| = 3\text{,}\) \(\|\vec B\| = 4\) and \(\|\vec A\times\vec B\| = 6\text{.}\) Find
    1. \(\left|\vec A\cdot\vec B\right|\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\left\|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec A}\vec B\right\|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Let \(\theta\) be the angle between the two vectors, \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\text{.}\) From \(\|\vec A\times\vec B\| = \|\vec A\|\,\|\vec B\|\sin\theta\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} 6 = (3)(4)\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\theta = \frac{6}{12} = \frac12 . \end{equation*}
    Since \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} |\cos\theta| = \sqrt{1 - \tfrac14} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} . \end{equation*}
    (We cannot determine the sign of \(\cos\theta\text{,}\) since \(\theta\) could be \(30^\circ\) or \(150^\circ\text{;}\) this is exactly why the problem asks for the absolute value.) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \left|\vec A\cdot\vec B\right| = \|\vec A\|\,\|\vec B\|\,|\cos\theta| = (3)(4)\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 6\sqrt3 . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (b) The projection is \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec A}\vec B = \left(\dfrac{\vec A\cdot\vec B}{\|\vec A\|^2}\right)\vec A\text{,}\) so its length is
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec A}\vec B\right\| = \frac{\left|\vec A\cdot\vec B\right|}{\|\vec A\|^2}\,\|\vec A\| = \frac{\left|\vec A\cdot\vec B\right|}{\|\vec A\|} = \frac{6\sqrt3}{3} = 2\sqrt3 . \end{equation*}
    As a sanity check, \(2\sqrt3 \approx 3.46\text{,}\) which is less than \(\|\vec B\| = 4\text{,}\) as a shadow must be.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Two vectors \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) are drawn with a common tail; \(\vec A\) points up and to the right, \(\vec B\) points up and to the left, and the angle between them is slightly larger than a right angle. Sketch and clearly label a diagram showing \(\vec A\text{,}\) \(\vec B\text{,}\) and \(\vec A \times \vec B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To make the picture concrete let us take \(\vec A = \left\langle 4, 2 \right\rangle\) and \(\vec B = \left\langle -3, 5 \right\rangle\text{,}\) which have roughly the directions described. Everything below is a statement about the geometry, so the same picture is correct for any pair of vectors in that configuration.
    🔗
    The cross product \(\vec A\times\vec B\) is perpendicular to both, hence perpendicular to the page. The right-hand rule (curl the fingers of the right hand from \(\vec A\) towards \(\vec B\) through the smaller angle) shows that it points out of the page, which we draw as a circle with a dot in it. Indeed, treating the vectors as lying in the \(xy\)-plane,
    \begin{equation*} \vec A\times\vec B = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 4 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ -3 \amp 5 \amp 0 \end{vmatrix} = \left\langle 0,\ 0,\ (4)(5)-(2)(-3) \right\rangle = 26\,\mathbf k , \end{equation*}
    and the \(\mathbf k\)-component is positive. Its length, \(26\text{,}\) is the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\text{.}\) See Figure 22.28.
    🔗

    6. Circular Motion from a Perpendicular Force.

    Through this guided exercise, we prove that a force of constant magnitude that always points perpendicular to the velocity drives the particle around a circle. We then apply it to a charged particle in a magnetic field.
    🔗
    Both of the products you have met in this chapter appear, and they do genuinely different jobs. Watch for them:
    • the dot product is used, through \(\vec F\cdot\vec v\text{,}\) to determine whether the speed of a particle acted upon by a perpendicular force can change,
      🔗
      🔗
    • the cross product is used to determine the direction and magnitude of the force that keeps a charged particle moving in a uniform magnetic field.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    A particle of mass \(m\) moves under a force \(\vec F\) of constant magnitude that is always perpendicular to its velocity \(\vec v\text{.}\) Show that the particle’s speed is constant.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\vec F\) is perpendicular to \(\vec v\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \vec F\cdot\vec v = 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now consider the square of the speed, \(v^2 = \vec v\cdot\vec v\text{.}\) Using the product rule for the dot product
     1 
    The product rule holds for the dot product just as it does for ordinary multiplication: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\vec u\cdot\vec v\right) = \dfrac{d\vec u}{dt}\cdot\vec v + \vec u\cdot\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt}\text{.}\) Taking \(\vec u = \vec v\) makes the two terms equal, which gives the step used here, \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\vec v\cdot\vec v\right) = 2\,\vec v\cdot\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt}\text{.}\)
    and then Newton’s second law \(m\,\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt} = \vec F\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dt}\left(v^2\right) \amp = \frac{d}{dt} \left(\vec v\cdot\vec v\right) = 2\,\vec v\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt}\\ \amp = \frac{2}{m}\,\vec v\cdot\vec F = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    So \(v^2\) never changes, and hence neither does the speed \(v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Why the dot product is the right tool here. The quantity \(\vec F\cdot\vec v\) is the rate at which the force does work on the particle. A force with a forward component speeds the particle up; a force with a backward component slows it down. Only the part of \(\vec F\) lying along \(\vec v\) can change the speed, and the dot product is exactly the mathematical tool that isolates that part. When it vanishes, can only change the direction of the velocity but not its magnitude, and so can only turn the particle, while the speed remains constant. See Figure 22.29.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    During a short time interval \(\Delta t\text{,}\) show that the direction of the velocity turns through the angle
    \begin{equation*} \Delta\theta = \frac{F}{mv}\,\Delta t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    (Hint: draw the two velocity vectors, which have equal length, from a common tail.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the previous task the velocity never changes length; it only swings around. So \(\vec v(t)\) and \(\vec v(t+\Delta t)\) are two vectors of the same length \(v\text{,}\) separated by the small angle \(\Delta\theta\) we are after. Drawn from a common tail they form an isosceles triangle whose third side is \(\Delta\vec v = \vec v(t+\Delta t) - \vec v(t)\text{,}\) as in Figure 22.30.
    🔗
    The tips of the two vectors both lie on a circle of radius \(v\text{.}\) The straight side \(\Delta\vec v\) is the chord cutting off the arc of angle \(\Delta\theta\) on that circle, and for a small angle a chord is very nearly the arc it subtends:
    \begin{equation*} \left|\Delta\vec v\right| = 2v\sin\frac{\Delta\theta}{2} \approx v\,\Delta\theta\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    On the other hand Newton’s second law over the short interval gives
    \begin{equation*} \left|\Delta\vec v\right| \approx \frac{F}{m}\,\Delta t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Equating the two expressions for \(\left|\Delta\vec v\right|\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} v\,\Delta\theta = \frac{F}{m}\,\Delta t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta\theta = \frac{F}{mv}\,\Delta t\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which is what we wanted. Notice the rate of turning \(\Delta\theta/\Delta t = F/(mv)\) is a constant, since \(F\text{,}\) \(m\) and \(v\) all are: the particle swings around at a steady angular rate.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Combine \(\Delta s = v\,\Delta t\) with the arc-length formula \(\Delta s = R\,\Delta\theta\) to prove that
    \begin{equation*} R = \frac{mv^2}{F}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    In time \(\Delta t\) the particle covers the arc length \(\Delta s = v\,\Delta t\text{.}\) If that arc bends through the angle \(\Delta\theta\text{,}\) then the radius \(R\) of the arc is defined by \(\Delta s = R\,\Delta\theta\text{.}\) Solving for \(R\) and substituting both expressions,
    \begin{equation*} R = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta\theta} = \frac{v\,\Delta t}{\dfrac{F}{mv}\,\Delta t} = \frac{mv^2}{F}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Conclude that the trajectory is a circle.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The quantity \(R = mv^2/F\) is built from \(m\text{,}\) \(v\) and \(F\text{,}\) every one of which is constant — \(v\) because of the first task, and \(F\) by hypothesis. So the path bends through equal angles over equal arc lengths, everywhere along the trajectory: its radius of curvature is the same constant \(R\) at every point.
    🔗
    A plane curve with constant radius of curvature is a circle. Concretely, the centre of the turn always lies a fixed distance \(R\) from the particle in the direction of \(\vec F\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    A particle of charge \(q\) enters a uniform magnetic field \(\vec B\) with its velocity perpendicular to the field. The magnetic force is
    \begin{equation*} \vec F_B = q\,\vec v\times\vec B\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Show that this force has magnitude \(|q|\,vB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The length of a cross product is \(\left|\vec v\times\vec B\right| = vB\sin\alpha\text{,}\) where \(\alpha\) is the angle between \(\vec v\) and \(\vec B\text{.}\) Here the velocity is perpendicular to the field, so \(\alpha = 90^\circ\) and \(\sin\alpha = 1\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \left|\vec F_B\right| = |q|\left|\vec v\times\vec B\right| = |q|\,vB\sin 90^\circ = |q|\,vB\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since \(v\) is constant (we are about to confirm this) and \(B\) is uniform, this magnitude is constant — the first hypothesis of part a.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Explain why this force is always perpendicular to the velocity — not just at the initial instant, but at every moment of the motion.
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is the defining property of the cross product: \(\vec v\times\vec B\) is perpendicular to both factors, whatever they happen to be. So \(\vec F_B = q\,\vec v\times\vec B\) is perpendicular to \(\vec v\) automatically, at every instant, with no computation and no special assumption about where the particle is or which way it is heading.
    🔗
    If you would like it verified rather than quoted, the dot product does it in one line.
     2 
    Two facts are doing the work. First, the scalar triple product \(\left(\vec a\times\vec b\right)\cdot\vec c\) equals the determinant whose rows are \(\vec a\text{,}\) \(\vec b\) and \(\vec c\text{.}\) Second, a determinant with two equal rows is zero, since interchanging those two rows both leaves the determinant unchanged and reverses its sign.
    Taking \(\vec c = \vec v\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec F_B\cdot\vec v = q\left(\vec v\times\vec B\right)\cdot\vec v = q\begin{vmatrix} v_1 \amp v_2 \amp v_3 \\ B_1 \amp B_2 \amp B_3 \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp v_3 \end{vmatrix} = 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So such a magnetic field can never change the speed of a charged particle, no matter how strong it is. Here the magnetic force does no work and it solely changes the direction of the velocity.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    Deduce that the particle moves in a circle, and find the radius of that circle.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The previous two tasks establish exactly the two hypotheses of part a: the magnetic force has constant magnitude \(|q|vB\text{,}\) and it is always perpendicular to the velocity. The argument of parts a–d therefore applies verbatim, and the particle travels in a circle at constant speed.
    🔗
    For the radius, put \(F = |q|vB\) into \(R = mv^2/F\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} r = \frac{mv^2}{|q|vB} = \frac{mv}{|q|B}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Equivalently, the magnetic force supplies precisely the inward force \(mv^2/r\) that circular motion demands:
    \begin{equation*} |q|vB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r = \frac{mv}{|q|B}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    See Figure 22.32. Faster particles and heavier particles travel on wider circles; stronger fields and larger charges wind them tighter.
