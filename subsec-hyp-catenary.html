    Subsection 1.5.1 *Hanging Cables and the Catenary

    Starred section. This one is for the interested reader. It will not be examined.
    🔗
    If a heavy flexible cable (such as a telephone line, a power line, or a chain) hangs freely from two supports, it settles into a curve called a catenary. Contrary to a common guess, this curve is not a parabola; it is the graph of a hyperbolic cosine,
    \begin{equation*} y = a\cosh\left(\frac{x}{a}\right), \end{equation*}
    where \(x\) is measured horizontally from the lowest point of the cable, so that the \(y\)-axis is the axis of symmetry and the lowest point sits at height \(y(0) = a\text{.}\) Sliding the curve up or down, as in \(y = c + a\cosh(x/a)\text{,}\) only changes where we draw the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    The shape comes out of a balance of forces. Look at the piece of cable running from the lowest point to a point where the cable makes an angle \(\theta\) with the horizontal, and let \(s\) be the arc length of that piece. Three forces act on it: the tension \(T_0\) at the lowest point, which is horizontal; the tension \(T\) along the cable at the other end; and the weight \(\lambda g s\) of the piece, where \(\lambda\) is the mass per unit length; see Figure 1.5.1. Balancing the horizontal and the vertical components gives
    \begin{align*} T\cos\theta \amp = T_0\\ T\sin\theta \amp = \lambda g s. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The quantity \(a = T_0/(\lambda g)\) has units of length, and dividing the second equation by the first eliminates \(T\) and leaves \(s = a\tan\theta\text{.}\) Since the cable makes the angle \(\theta\) with the horizontal, \(\tan\theta\) is its slope, so
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \tan\theta = \frac{s}{a}. \end{equation*}
    Now use the arc length element \(ds = \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (s/a)^2}\,dx\) and separate the variables:
    \begin{equation*} dx = \frac{a\,ds}{\sqrt{a^2+s^2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x = a\sinh^{-1}\!\left(\frac{s}{a}\right), \end{equation*}
    where the constant of integration vanishes because \(s = 0\) at \(x = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solving for \(s\) gives \(s = a\sinh(x/a)\text{,}\) and therefore
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{s}{a} = \sinh\left(\frac{x}{a}\right). \end{equation*}
    One last integration, together with \(y(0) = a\text{,}\) produces the catenary equation \(y = a\cosh(x/a)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Two things are worth noticing. First, \(a\) is a pure scale factor: written as \(y/a = \cosh(x/a)\text{,}\) the equation shows that every catenary is a scaled copy of the single curve \(y = \cosh x\text{,}\) in exactly the same way that every circle is a scaled copy of the unit circle; Figure 1.5.2 shows four of them. Second, \(a\) is fixed by the cable itself. If the cable has half-length \(l\) and its two supports are a distance \(2b\) apart, then putting \(s = l\) at \(x = b\) in \(s = a\sinh(x/a)\) gives
    \begin{equation*} l = a\sinh\left(\frac{b}{a}\right), \end{equation*}
    which determines \(a\) (numerically) from the two lengths. Since \(a = T_0/(\lambda g)\text{,}\) a cable pulled tight has a large \(a\) and hangs almost flat, while a slack one has a small \(a\) and sags sharply.
    🔗
    A worked example of a hanging cable, in which we find the slope of the cable and the angle at which it meets its pole, appears in Example 1.6.1.
    🔗
    The derivation above follows F. Behroozi, In Praise of the Catenary, The Physics Teacher 56, 214–217 (2018), which also discusses the sense in which all catenaries are similar to one another and suggests simple classroom demonstrations.
