    Worksheet 22.7 Exercises for The Dot Product
    

    These problems use the projection of one vector onto another. If you would like to review this topic before attempting them, see The Dot Product.
    🔗

    1.

    Consider the two vectors \(\mathbf u = 3\,\mathbf j + 4\,\mathbf k\) and \(\mathbf v = \mathbf i + \mathbf j\text{.}\) Write \(\mathbf u\) as the sum of a vector parallel to \(\mathbf v\) and a vector orthogonal to \(\mathbf v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have \(\vec u \cdot \vec v = 3\) and \(|\vec v| = \sqrt{1+1} = \sqrt2\text{.}\) The component of \(\vec u\) parallel to \(\vec v\) is
    \begin{equation*} \vec u_{\parallel} = \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v} \vec u = \left(\frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\right)\vec v = \frac32\,(\mathbf i + \mathbf j) = \frac32\,\mathbf i + \frac32\,\mathbf j, \end{equation*}
    and the component orthogonal to \(\vec v\) is
    \begin{equation*} \vec u_{\perp} = \vec u - \vec u_{\parallel} = \left(3\,\mathbf j + 4\,\mathbf k\right) - \left(\frac32\,\mathbf i + \frac32\,\mathbf j\right) = -\frac32\,\mathbf i + \frac32\,\mathbf j + 4\,\mathbf k. \end{equation*}
    Here \(\vec u_{\parallel}\) is parallel to \(\vec v\) and \(\vec u_{\perp}\) is orthogonal to \(\vec v\text{.}\) Note that \(\vec u_{\parallel} + \vec u_{\perp} = \vec u\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Determine whether the following pairs of vectors are orthogonal, parallel, or neither.
    1. \(\vec u_1 = -3\,\mathbf i + 9\,\mathbf j + 6\,\mathbf k\) and \(\vec u_2 = 4\,\mathbf i - 12\,\mathbf j - 8\,\mathbf k\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\vec v_1 = \mathbf i + \mathbf j - 2\,\mathbf k\) and \(\vec v_2 = \mathbf i + \mathbf j + \mathbf k\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\vec w_1 = a\,\mathbf i + b\,\mathbf j\) and \(\vec w_2 = c\,\mathbf k\text{,}\) where \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) are non-zero real numbers.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Parallel, since \(\vec u_2 = -\frac43\,\vec u_1\text{:}\) indeed \(-\frac43\langle -3, 9, 6\rangle = \langle 4, -12, -8\rangle\text{.}\) (Two vectors are parallel exactly when one is a scalar multiple of the other.)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Orthogonal, since \(\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2 = 1 + 1 - 2 = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Orthogonal, since \(\vec w_1 \cdot \vec w_2 = \langle a, b, 0\rangle \cdot \langle 0, 0, c\rangle = 0\text{.}\) They are not parallel, because \(\vec w_2\) has a non-zero \(\mathbf k\) component while \(\vec w_1\) does not.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Find the projection of \(-\mathbf i + 5\,\mathbf j - 2\,\mathbf k\) on \(3\,\mathbf i - \mathbf j\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(\vec u = \langle -1, 5, -2\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \langle 3, -1, 0\rangle\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec u \cdot \vec v = -3 - 5 + 0 = -8, \qquad |\vec v|^2 = 9 + 1 + 0 = 10, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{align*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec v}\vec u \amp = \left(\frac{\vec u \cdot \vec v}{|\vec v|^2}\right)\vec v = -\frac{8}{10}\langle 3, -1, 0\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle -\frac{12}{5}, \frac45, 0\right\rangle = -\frac{12}{5}\,\mathbf i + \frac45\,\mathbf j. \end{align*}
    The negative scalar tells us the projection points opposite to \(\vec v\text{,}\) i.e. the angle between \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) is obtuse.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    A 100-meter dash is run on a track in the direction of the vector \(2\,\mathbf i + 6\,\mathbf j\text{.}\) The wind velocity is \(\mathbf v = 5\,\mathbf i + 2\,\mathbf j\) km/hr. The rules say that a legal wind speed measured in the direction of the dash must not exceed 5 km/hr.
    1. Will the race results be disqualified due to an illegal wind? Justify your answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the velocity vector of the wind in the direction of the dash.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\vec d = \langle 2, 6\rangle\) be the direction of the dash and \(\vec v = \langle 5, 2\rangle\) the wind velocity. Then
    \begin{equation*} \vec v \cdot \vec d = 10 + 12 = 22, \qquad |\vec d| = \sqrt{4 + 36} = \sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}. \end{equation*}
    1. The wind speed measured in the direction of the dash is the scalar component of \(\vec v\) along \(\vec d\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \operatorname{comp}_{\vec d}\vec v = \frac{\vec v \cdot \vec d}{|\vec d|} = \frac{22}{2\sqrt{10}} = \frac{11}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 3.48 \text{ km/hr}. \end{equation*}
      Since \(3.48 \lt 5\text{,}\) the wind is legal and the race results will not be disqualified.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The velocity vector of the wind in the direction of the dash is the vector projection
      \begin{align*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec d}\vec v \amp = \left(\frac{\vec v \cdot \vec d}{|\vec d|^2}\right)\vec d = \frac{22}{40}\langle 2, 6\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle \frac{11}{10}, \frac{33}{10}\right\rangle = 1.1\,\mathbf i + 3.3\,\mathbf j \text{ km/hr}. \end{align*}
      As a check, its length is \(\sqrt{1.1^2 + 3.3^2} = 1.1\sqrt{10} = \frac{11}{\sqrt{10}}\text{,}\) matching part A.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Let \(\vec v\) be the vector from \((-4,3,-1)\) to \((-2,-1,3)\text{.}\)
    1. Find a non-zero unit vector that is orthogonal to \(\vec v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the angle between \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w = 3\mathbf i - \mathbf k\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First,
    \begin{align*} \vec v \amp = \left\langle -2-(-4),\ -1-3,\ 3-(-1) \right\rangle = \left\langle 2,\ -4,\ 4 \right\rangle ,\\ \|\vec v\| \amp = \sqrt{4+16+16} = \sqrt{36} = 6 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    (a) We need any non-zero \(\vec u\) with \(\vec u \cdot \vec v = 0\text{.}\) Writing \(\vec u = \left\langle a,b,c\right\rangle\text{,}\) the condition is
    \begin{equation*} 2a - 4b + 4c = 0 . \end{equation*}
    This is one equation in three unknowns, so there are infinitely many answers; we just pick a convenient one. Taking \(c = 0\) gives \(2a = 4b\text{,}\) so \(a = 2\text{,}\) \(b = 1\) works:
    \begin{equation*} \vec u = \left\langle 2,\ 1,\ 0 \right\rangle , \qquad \vec u \cdot \vec v = 4 - 4 + 0 = 0 . \end{equation*}
    Finally we normalize:
    \begin{equation*} \hat u = \frac{\vec u}{\|\vec u\|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\left\langle 2,\ 1,\ 0 \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{2}{\sqrt5},\ \frac{1}{\sqrt5},\ 0 \right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (b) With \(\vec w = \left\langle 3, 0, -1 \right\rangle\) we have
    \begin{align*} \vec v \cdot \vec w \amp = (2)(3) + (-4)(0) + (4)(-1) = 6 - 4 = 2 ,\\ \|\vec w\| \amp = \sqrt{9+0+1} = \sqrt{10} . \end{align*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \cos\theta = \frac{\vec v \cdot \vec w}{\|\vec v\|\,\|\vec w\|} = \frac{2}{6\sqrt{10}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{10}} \approx 0.1054 , \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{3\sqrt{10}}\right) \approx 1.465 \text{ radians} \approx 83.9^{\circ} . \end{equation*}
    The angle is slightly less than a right angle, which matches the fact that the dot product is positive but small.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Let \(A = (0,0,1)\) and \(B = (0,2,3)\text{.}\) Find an equation for the set of all points \(P = (x,y,z)\) such that \(\vec{AP}\) is orthogonal to \(\vec{BP}\text{.}\) Then sketch the resulting surface in three dimensions.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The two vectors are
    \begin{align*} \vec{AP} \amp = \left\langle x-0,\ y-0,\ z-1 \right\rangle = \left\langle x,\ y,\ z-1 \right\rangle ,\\ \vec{BP} \amp = \left\langle x,\ y-2,\ z-3 \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Orthogonality means the dot product vanishes:
    \begin{align*} \vec{AP}\cdot\vec{BP} \amp = 0\\ x^2 + y(y-2) + (z-1)(z-3) \amp = 0\\ x^2 + y^2 - 2y + z^2 - 4z + 3 \amp = 0 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Now complete the square in \(y\) and in \(z\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} x^2 + \left(y^2 - 2y + 1\right) + \left(z^2 - 4z + 4\right) \amp = -3 + 1 + 4\\ x^2 + (y-1)^2 + (z-2)^2 \amp = 2 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is the sphere of radius \(\sqrt2\) centred at \((0,1,2)\text{.}\) That centre is exactly the midpoint of \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) and the radius is exactly half of \(\|\vec{AB}\| = \|\langle 0,2,2\rangle\| = 2\sqrt2\text{.}\) This is the three-dimensional version of the familiar fact from plane geometry: the points from which a given segment subtends a right angle form the circle having that segment as a diameter. See Figure 22.24.
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    Two vectors \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) are drawn with a common tail; \(\vec A\) points up and to the right, \(\vec B\) points up and to the left, and the angle between them is slightly larger than a right angle. Sketch and clearly label a diagram showing \(\vec A\text{,}\) \(\vec B\text{,}\) and \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec A}\vec B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To make the picture concrete let us take \(\vec A = \left\langle 4, 2 \right\rangle\) and \(\vec B = \left\langle -3, 5 \right\rangle\text{,}\) which have roughly the directions described. Everything below is a statement about the geometry, so the same picture is correct for any pair of vectors in that configuration.
    🔗
    The projection of \(\vec B\) onto \(\vec A\) is the shadow that \(\vec B\) casts on the line through \(\vec A\text{,}\) obtained by dropping a perpendicular from the tip of \(\vec B\) to that line. Algebraically,
    \begin{align*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec A}\vec B \amp = \left(\frac{\vec A\cdot\vec B}{\|\vec A\|^2}\right)\vec A = \left(\frac{-12+10}{20}\right)\left\langle 4,2\right\rangle\\ \amp = -\frac{1}{10}\left\langle 4,2\right\rangle = \left\langle -0.4,\ -0.2 \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    Because the angle between \(\vec A\) and \(\vec B\) is obtuse, the dot product is negative and the projection points in the direction opposite to \(\vec A\text{.}\) This is the feature of the picture the problem is testing. See Figure 22.25.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8.

    Suppose \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are non-zero vectors satisfying
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf u + \mathbf v| = 5, \qquad |\mathbf u - \mathbf v| = 13, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf u|^2 + |\mathbf v|^2 = 97, \qquad |\mathbf u| = 7 . \end{equation*}
    Without finding the coordinates of either vector:
    1. Compute \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find \(|\mathbf v|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Determine whether the angle is acute, right, or obtuse.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The whole problem rests on one idea: the square of a length is a dot product, \(|\mathbf w|^2 = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf w\text{.}\) Expanding with the distributive property,
    \begin{align*} |\mathbf u + \mathbf v|^2 \amp = (\mathbf u + \mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = |\mathbf u|^2 + 2\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v + |\mathbf v|^2 ,\\ |\mathbf u - \mathbf v|^2 \amp = (\mathbf u - \mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u - \mathbf v) = |\mathbf u|^2 - 2\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v + |\mathbf v|^2 . \end{align*}
    These two identities are the algebraic form of the parallelogram in Figure 22.26: the diagonals of the parallelogram built on \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are \(\mathbf u + \mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf u - \mathbf v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Subtracting the second identity from the first makes the \(|\mathbf u|^2\) and \(|\mathbf v|^2\) terms cancel, leaving only the dot product:
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf u + \mathbf v|^2 - |\mathbf u - \mathbf v|^2 = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v . \end{equation*}
    Substituting the two given lengths,
    \begin{align*} 5^2 - 13^2 \amp = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v ,\\ 25 - 169 \amp = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v ,\\ -144 \amp = 4\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v , \end{align*}
    and therefore \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = -36\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It is worth pausing to check that the data are consistent. Adding the two identities instead of subtracting gives the parallelogram law,
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf u + \mathbf v|^2 + |\mathbf u - \mathbf v|^2 = 2\left(|\mathbf u|^2 + |\mathbf v|^2\right) , \end{equation*}
    and indeed \(5^2 + 13^2 = 25 + 169 = 194 = 2(97)\text{,}\) exactly as it should be.
    🔗
    (b) We are told \(|\mathbf u| = 7\) and \(|\mathbf u|^2 + |\mathbf v|^2 = 97\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} 7^2 + |\mathbf v|^2 \amp = 97 ,\\ 49 + |\mathbf v|^2 \amp = 97 ,\\ |\mathbf v|^2 \amp = 48 . \end{align*}
    A length is never negative, so \(|\mathbf v| = \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt3 \approx 6.93\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) For non-zero vectors, \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = |\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|\cos\theta\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} \cos\theta \amp = \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{|\mathbf u|\,|\mathbf v|} = \frac{-36}{(7)\left(4\sqrt3\right)}\\ \amp = -\frac{9}{7\sqrt3} = -\frac{3\sqrt3}{7} \approx -0.7423 , \end{align*}
    and therefore
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(-\frac{3\sqrt3}{7}\right) \approx 2.407 \text{ radians} \approx 137.9^{\circ} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (d) Since \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = -36 \lt 0\) and the lengths are positive, \(\cos\theta \lt 0\text{.}\) Hence \(\tfrac{\pi}{2} \lt \theta \lt \pi\) and the angle is obtuse. Notice that we could have answered this part immediately after part (a), without knowing either length: the sign of the dot product already decides the type of angle.
    🔗
    A remark on the data. The two diagonal lengths by themselves already determine \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\text{,}\) and the condition \(|\mathbf u|^2 + |\mathbf v|^2 = 97\) then follows automatically from the parallelogram law. So the individual lengths are not pinned down by that data alone: the pair \(|\mathbf u| = 4\sqrt3\text{,}\) \(|\mathbf v| = 7\) fits equally well and gives the same angle, while other splits of \(97\) give different angles. This is why the extra condition \(|\mathbf u| = 7\) is needed for parts (b) and (c).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    A constant force is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \left\langle 2, -1, 3 \right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    A particle is constrained to move in directions parallel to the plane
    \begin{equation*} 2x - y + 2z = 0 . \end{equation*}
    For a displacement of one unit, the work done by the force is \(W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf d\) is an allowed unit direction lying in the plane.
    1. Find the unit direction in the plane that maximizes the work.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the maximum possible work per unit distance.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find one unit direction in the plane for which the work is zero.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Reading the coefficients of the plane, a normal vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf n = \left\langle 2, -1, 2 \right\rangle , \qquad |\mathbf n| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3 . \end{equation*}
    A vector is parallel to the plane exactly when it is orthogonal to \(\mathbf n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Decompose the force into a normal piece and a piece lying in the plane,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F + \mathbf F_{\parallel} . \end{equation*}
    The first piece comes from the projection formula. Since
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n \amp = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = 2(2) + (-1)(-1) + 3(2)\\ \amp = 4 + 1 + 6 = 11 , \end{align*}
    and \(|\mathbf n|^2 = 9\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F = \frac{11}{9}\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{22}{9},\ -\frac{11}{9},\ \frac{22}{9}\right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    Subtracting,
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F_{\parallel} \amp = \mathbf F - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle - \left\langle \frac{22}{9},\ -\frac{11}{9},\ \frac{22}{9}\right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle -\frac49,\ \frac29,\ \frac59\right\rangle = \frac19\left\langle -4,\ 2,\ 5 \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    As a check, this really is parallel to the plane: \(\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = -8 - 2 + 10 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let \(\mathbf d\) be any allowed unit direction. Because \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F\) is orthogonal to every vector lying in the plane,
    \begin{equation*} W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d = \left(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F + \mathbf F_{\parallel}\right)\cdot\mathbf d = \mathbf F_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf d . \end{equation*}
    The normal part of the force is simply wasted: it presses the particle against the constraint and does no work. Writing \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf d = |\mathbf F_{\parallel}|\,|\mathbf d|\cos\varphi = |\mathbf F_{\parallel}|\cos\varphi\text{,}\) the largest value occurs when \(\cos\varphi = 1\text{,}\) that is when \(\mathbf d\) points in the same direction as \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\text{.}\) Since
    \begin{equation*} \left|\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\right| = \sqrt{16+4+25} = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5 , \end{equation*}
    the maximizing unit vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d_{\max} = \frac{\left\langle -4,\ 2,\ 5\right\rangle}{3\sqrt5} . \end{equation*}
    See Figure 22.27.
    🔗
    (b) The maximum work per unit displacement is the length of the in-plane component,
    \begin{align*} W_{\max} \amp = \left|\mathbf F_{\parallel}\right| = \left|\frac19\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\right|\\ \amp = \frac19\left(3\sqrt5\right) = \frac{\sqrt5}{3} \approx 0.745 . \end{align*}
    We can confirm this directly from the original force:
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d_{\max} \amp = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle\cdot \frac{\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{-8-2+15}{3\sqrt5}\\ \amp = \frac{5}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{\sqrt5}{3} . \end{align*}
    Compare this with \(|\mathbf F| = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.74\text{:}\) the constraint costs us about \(80\%\) of the force.
    🔗
    (c) We want a unit vector \(\mathbf d = \left\langle x,y,z\right\rangle\) satisfying two conditions at once: it must lie in the plane, and it must do no work. That is,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf F = 0 , \end{equation*}
    or explicitly
    \begin{align*} 2x - y + 2z \amp = 0 ,\\ 2x - y + 3z \amp = 0 . \end{align*}
    Subtracting the first equation from the second gives \(z = 0\text{,}\) and then \(2x - y = 0\text{,}\) so \(y = 2x\text{.}\) Taking \(x = 1\) gives the non-zero vector \(\left\langle 1,2,0\right\rangle\text{,}\) whose length is \(\sqrt{1+4} = \sqrt5\text{.}\) Hence one suitable unit vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\left\langle 1,\ 2,\ 0\right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    Indeed \(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2 - 2 + 0) = 0\text{,}\) and the opposite direction \(-\mathbf d_0\) works equally well. Geometrically, \(\mathbf d_0\) is the direction in the plane perpendicular to \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\text{:}\) moving along it, the particle travels neither with nor against the usable part of the force.
