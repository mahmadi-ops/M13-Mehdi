    1. True or False.

      If every directional derivative \(D_{\mathbf u}f(P_0)\) exists at a point \(P_0\text{,}\) then \(f\) must be continuous at \(P_0\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False, and the failure is dramatic. Let
      \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}\ \text{ for } (x,y)\ne(0,0), \qquad f(0,0) = 0 . \end{equation*}
      For a unit vector \(\mathbf u = \langle a,b\rangle\) with \(b \ne 0\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} D_{\mathbf u}f(0,0) = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(ta,tb)}{t} = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{a^2 b}{t^2a^4 + b^2} = \frac{a^2}{b}, \end{equation*}
      and \(D_{\mathbf u}f(0,0) = 0\) when \(b = 0\text{.}\) So every directional derivative exists. Yet along the parabola \(y = mx^2\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} f(x,mx^2) = \frac{m}{1+m^2}, \end{equation*}
      which depends on \(m\) — different parabolas approach different values, so \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f\) does not exist and \(f\) is not continuous at the origin. Existence of directional derivatives is a statement about behavior along straight lines only; continuity requires control along every approach.

    • False.

    • False, and the failure is dramatic. Let
      \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}\ \text{ for } (x,y)\ne(0,0), \qquad f(0,0) = 0 . \end{equation*}
      For a unit vector \(\mathbf u = \langle a,b\rangle\) with \(b \ne 0\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} D_{\mathbf u}f(0,0) = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(ta,tb)}{t} = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{a^2 b}{t^2a^4 + b^2} = \frac{a^2}{b}, \end{equation*}

    2. Which Identity Fails?

    Exactly one of the following is not valid for all vectors \(\vec a, \vec b, \vec c\) in \(\R^3\text{.}\) Which one?
    🔗
    • \(\vec a\cdot(\vec b\times\vec c) = (\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec c\)
    • This one is valid. Both sides equal the determinant with rows \(\vec a,\vec b,\vec c\text{,}\) so the dot and the cross may be interchanged.
    • \(\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c) = (\vec a\times\vec b)\times\vec c\)
    • Correct — the cross product is not associative. Take \(\vec a = \vec b = \mathbf i\) and \(\vec c = \mathbf j\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf i\times(\mathbf i\times\mathbf j) = \mathbf i\times\mathbf k = -\mathbf j, \qquad (\mathbf i\times\mathbf i)\times\mathbf j = \vec 0\times\mathbf j = \vec 0 . \end{equation*}
      The left side lies in the plane of \(\vec b\) and \(\vec c\text{;}\) the right side lies in the plane of \(\vec a\) and \(\vec b\text{.}\) There is no reason for those to agree.
    • \(\vec a\times(\vec b + \vec c) = \vec a\times\vec b + \vec a\times\vec c\)
    • This one is valid — the cross product is distributive over addition. (It is only associativity that fails.)
    • \(\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c) = (\vec a\cdot\vec c)\,\vec b - (\vec a\cdot\vec b)\,\vec c\)
    • This one is valid: it is the “BAC-CAB” triple product expansion. It looks the most exotic of the four, which is exactly why it is worth remembering that it is true.

    3. Distance Between Skew Lines.

    Consider the two lines
    \begin{align*} L_1: \amp \quad \vec r = \langle 1,0,2\rangle + t\,\langle 2,-1,1\rangle,\\ L_2: \amp \quad \vec r = \langle 0,3,-1\rangle + s\,\langle 1,2,-1\rangle . \end{align*}
    They are skew. Let \(\vec u,\vec v\) be the direction vectors and let \(\vec w\) join a point of \(L_1\) to a point of \(L_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The absolute value of the scalar triple product \(\left|\vec w\cdot(\vec u\times\vec v)\right|\) equals .
    🔗
    The distance between the two lines, to three decimal places, is .
    🔗
    Hint.
    The vector \(\vec u\times\vec v\) is perpendicular to both lines, so the distance is the length of the projection of \(\vec w\) onto it: \(d = \left|\vec w\cdot(\vec u\times\vec v)\right| / \left|\vec u\times\vec v\right|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(\vec u = \langle 2,-1,1\rangle\) and \(\vec v = \langle 1,2,-1\rangle\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec u\times\vec v = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 2 \amp -1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \end{vmatrix} = \langle -1,\, 3,\, 5\rangle, \qquad \left|\vec u\times\vec v\right| = \sqrt{35}. \end{equation*}
    Taking \(\vec w = \langle 0,3,-1\rangle - \langle 1,0,2\rangle = \langle -1,3,-3\rangle\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec w\cdot(\vec u\times\vec v) = 1 + 9 - 15 = -5, \qquad d = \frac{5}{\sqrt{35}} = \frac{\sqrt{35}}{7} \approx 0.845 . \end{equation*}

    4. Why a Magnetic Field Cannot Change a Particle’s Speed.

    Arrange the steps below into a correct proof that the magnetic force does no work, and therefore that a charged particle in a magnetic field moves at constant speed.
    🔗
    Let a particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) move with velocity \(\vec v\) through a magnetic field \(\vec B\text{.}\)

    
---


    The force on the particle is \(\vec F = q\,\vec v\times\vec B\text{.}\)

    
---


    By definition, a cross product is perpendicular to both of its factors, so \(\vec v\times\vec B\) is perpendicular to \(\vec v\text{.}\)

    
---


    Hence \(\vec F\cdot\vec v
= q\left(\vec v\times\vec B\right)\cdot\vec v = 0\text{.}\)

    
---


    The kinetic energy satisfies \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\,\vec v\cdot\vec v\right)
= m\,\vec v\cdot\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v\text{.}\)

    
---


    Therefore \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m v^2\right) = 0\text{,}\) so \(v\) is constant: the field changes the particle’s direction but never its speed.

    5. Which of These Are Hyperboloids?

    None of the equations below is in standard position. Complete the square where necessary, then select every equation whose surface is a hyperboloid, of one sheet or of two.
    🔗
    • \(x^2 + y^2 - z^2 - 2x = 0\)
    • Correct. Completing the square gives \((x-1)^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1\text{:}\) one negative square, equal to a positive constant, so a hyperboloid of one sheet.
    • \(x^2 + y^2 + 4z^2 - 4y = 0\)
    • No. This is \(x^2 + (y-2)^2 + 4z^2 = 4\) — every square is positive, so it is an ellipsoid.
    • \(x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = 1\)
    • Correct. Two negative squares against a positive constant gives a hyperboloid of two sheets.
    • \(x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0\)
    • No — and this is the trap. It has the same signs as the first option, but it equals \(0\) rather than a positive constant. That degenerates the hyperboloid into an elliptic cone, the surface the two families of hyperboloids approach asymptotically.
    • \(z + 1 = x^2 - y^2\)
    • No. The word “hyperbolic” is a red herring: this is linear in \(z\text{,}\) so it is a hyperbolic paraboloid (a saddle) with vertex at \((0,0,-1)\text{,}\) not a hyperboloid.
    Solution.
    Completing the square in the first and second,
    \begin{align*} x^2 + y^2 - z^2 - 2x \amp = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-1)^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1,\\ x^2 + y^2 + 4z^2 - 4y \amp = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 + (y-2)^2 + 4z^2 = 4 . \end{align*}
    Reading off the signs: one negative square against \(+1\) is a hyperboloid of one sheet; two negative squares against \(+1\) is a hyperboloid of two sheets. So the first and third are hyperboloids. The second is an ellipsoid, the fourth equals \(0\) and so is a cone, and the fifth is linear in \(z\) and so is a hyperbolic paraboloid.

    6. Classify the Critical Points.

      The function \(f(x,y) = x^3 - 3x + y^3 - 3y\) has four critical points. Drag each one into the correct category.
      🔗
    • \((1,1)\)
    • Local minimum
    • \((-1,-1)\)
    • Local maximum
    • \((1,-1)\)
    • \((-1,1)\)
    • Saddle point
    Solution.
    From \(f_x = 3x^2 - 3 = 0\) and \(f_y = 3y^2 - 3 = 0\) we get \(x = \pm 1\) and \(y = \pm 1\text{,}\) giving four critical points. Since \(f_{xx} = 6x\text{,}\) \(f_{yy} = 6y\) and \(f_{xy} = 0\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 = 36xy . \end{equation*}
    So \(D = 36 \gt 0\) at \((1,1)\) and \((-1,-1)\text{,}\) while \(D = -36 \lt 0\) at \((1,-1)\) and \((-1,1)\) — both saddles. Among the two with \(D \gt 0\text{,}\) the sign of \(f_{xx}\) decides: \(f_{xx}(1,1) = 6 \gt 0\) gives a local minimum, and \(f_{xx}(-1,-1) = -6 \lt 0\) gives a local maximum.

    7. Find the Error.

    Below is a student’s computation of the derivative of \(f(x,y) = x^2 y\) at the point \((1,2)\) in the direction of \(\vec v = \langle 3,4\rangle\text{.}\) Exactly one line contains the error. Click on that line.
    🔗
    The error is in the third line. The formula \(D_{\mathbf u}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u\) requires a unit vector. Since \(\left|\vec v\right| = 5\text{,}\) the correct direction is \(\mathbf u = \left\langle \tfrac35, \tfrac45 \right\rangle\text{,}\) giving
    \begin{equation*} D_{\mathbf u}f(1,2) = 4\cdot\tfrac35 + 1\cdot\tfrac45 = \frac{16}{5} = 3.2, \end{equation*}
    not \(16\text{.}\) Lines 1, 2 and 4 are each correct given the line before them — line 4 faithfully reports the (wrong) number produced by line 3.
    \(\nabla f = \left\langle 2xy,\; x^2\right\rangle\)
    \(\nabla f(1,2) = \left\langle 4,\; 1\right\rangle\)
    \(D_{\vec v}f(1,2) = \nabla f(1,2)\cdot\vec v = (4)(3) + (1)(4) = 16\)
    So the directional derivative equals \(16\text{.}\)
