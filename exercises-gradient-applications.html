    Exercises 18.8 Exercises: Scalar Fields and Potentials

    Each exercise below repeats the three questions of Example 18.24 in a different physical setting: find the direction of most rapid decrease, the rate along a prescribed direction, and the directions of no change.
    🔗

    1. Electric Potential on a Coated Plate.

    The electric potential on a thin coated plate, in volts, is
    \begin{equation*} V(x,y) = 100 - 2x^2 - 6y^2, \end{equation*}
    with \(x\) and \(y\) in centimeters. Consider the point \(P_0(2,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Find \(\nabla V\) at \(P_0\) and the electric field \(\mathbf E = -\nabla V\) there. Along which unit vector does the potential drop most rapidly, and at what rate?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A test charge is nudged along \(\mathbf u = \tfrac{4}{5}\mathbf i - \tfrac{3}{5}\mathbf j\text{.}\) Compute \(D_{\mathbf u}V\) at \(P_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the two unit vectors along which the potential does not change, and name the curve they are tangent to.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    1. \(\nabla V = -4x\,\mathbf i - 12y\,\mathbf j\text{,}\) so \((\nabla V)_{(2,1)} = -8\,\mathbf i - 12\,\mathbf j\) and \(\mathbf E = 8\,\mathbf i + 12\,\mathbf j\) V/cm. The potential drops fastest along \(\mathbf u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{13}}(2\,\mathbf i + 3\,\mathbf j)\text{,}\) at the rate \(-|\nabla V| = -4\sqrt{13} \approx -14.4\) V/cm.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \begin{equation*} D_{\mathbf u}V = (-8)\left(\tfrac{4}{5}\right) + (-12)\left(-\tfrac{3}{5}\right) = \tfrac{4}{5} = 0.8 \end{equation*}
      in V/cm. The potential rises slightly along this heading.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\mathbf u = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{13}}(3\,\mathbf i - 2\,\mathbf j)\text{.}\) These are tangent to the equipotential ellipse \(2x^2 + 6y^2 = 14\) through \(P_0\text{,}\) and they are orthogonal to \(\mathbf E\text{,}\) as they must be.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. The Fall Line on a Ski Slope.

    A mountainside has elevation, in feet,
    \begin{equation*} h(x,y) = 3000 - 0.0004x^2 - 0.001y^2, \end{equation*}
    with \(x\) and \(y\) in feet. A skier stands at \(P_0(200,100)\text{.}\) Her gravitational potential energy is \(U = mgh\text{,}\) so the downhill force she feels is a positive multiple of \(-\nabla h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Find the fall line, the unit vector of steepest descent, and the grade of the slope there, expressed as a percent (feet dropped per hundred feet travelled).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A traverse is a route on which she neither climbs nor descends. Find the two unit vectors that accomplish this.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. She wants a gentler line of exactly \(12\%\) grade. Using \(D_{\mathbf u}h = |\nabla h|\cos\theta\text{,}\) find the angle \(\theta\) between her heading and the fall line.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    1. \(\nabla h = -0.0008x\,\mathbf i - 0.002y\,\mathbf j\text{,}\) so \((\nabla h)_{(200,100)} = -0.16\,\mathbf i - 0.20\,\mathbf j\text{.}\) Steepest descent is along \(\mathbf u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{41}}(4\,\mathbf i + 5\,\mathbf j)\text{,}\) and \(|\nabla h| = \sqrt{0.0656} \approx 0.256\text{,}\) a grade of about \(25.6\%\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\mathbf u = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{41}}(5\,\mathbf i - 4\,\mathbf j)\text{,}\) tangent to the contour line of constant elevation through \(P_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\cos\theta = 0.12/0.256 \approx 0.469\text{,}\) so \(\theta \approx 62^\circ\) off the fall line, on either side.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Sailing the Isobars of a Low.

    Near the center of a low-pressure system the sea-level pressure, in millibars, is modeled by
    \begin{equation*} P(x,y) = 988 + 0.004\left(x^2 + y^2\right), \end{equation*}
    with \(x\) and \(y\) in miles from the center of the low. A ship sits at \(P_0(20,10)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Find the pressure at the ship and \(\nabla P\) there. The pressure-gradient force per unit volume of air is a positive multiple of \(-\nabla P\text{;}\) in which unit direction does it push, and what is \(|\nabla P|\) in millibars per mile?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A second ship 40 miles from the center reports a stronger wind. Using this model, explain why \(|\nabla P|\) supports that report.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The captain decides to hold a constant barometer reading. Give the two possible unit headings at \(P_0\text{,}\) and describe the shape of the full route.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    1. \(P(20,10) = 988 + 0.004(500) = 990\) mb, and \(\nabla P = 0.008x\,\mathbf i + 0.008y\,\mathbf j\) gives \((\nabla P)_{(20,10)} = 0.16\,\mathbf i + 0.08\,\mathbf j\text{.}\) The force points along \(-\mathbf u = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(2\,\mathbf i + \mathbf j)\text{,}\) that is, inward toward the low, and \(|\nabla P| = 0.08\sqrt{5} \approx 0.18\) mb/mi.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Here \(|\nabla P| = 0.008\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) grows linearly with distance from the center, so at 40 miles it is \(0.32\) mb/mi, nearly double the value at the first ship. (Real storms reverse this trend close to the eye; the model is only reasonable over a limited annulus.)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\mathbf u = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(\mathbf i - 2\,\mathbf j)\text{.}\) Since the level curves of \(P\) are the circles \(x^2+y^2 = \text{constant}\text{,}\) holding the barometer steady means circling the storm at a fixed radius of \(\sqrt{500} \approx 22.4\) miles.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
